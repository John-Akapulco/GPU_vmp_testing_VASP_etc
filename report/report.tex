\title{Performance of GPU-Accelerated VASP 6.5.1 on a Dual NVIDIA H100 Node:\\
Materials Project PBE and r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN Workflows, Finite-Displacement Phonons,\\
and Concurrent Runs on One GPU}
\author{Gilles Frapper \\ Professor, Theoretical Chemistry \\ IC2MP UMR 7285 CNRS, Universit\'e de Poitiers \\ \href{mailto:gilles.frapper@univ-poitiers.fr}{gilles.frapper@univ-poitiers.fr}}
\date{Draft, September 28, 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We benchmark the GPU (OpenACC) build of VASP 6.5.1 on a shared node with two NVIDIA H100 NVL
GPUs. Single-point and full cell relaxation calculations are run on supercell series of Si, Al,
and MgO from 1 to 64 atoms, using the Materials Project PBE and r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN input
sets. We report wall time, time per self-consistent step, GPU memory, the cost of
r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN relative to PBE, the speedup from one to two GPUs with two
parallelization strategies, run-to-run reproducibility, and agreement with published Materials
Project energies. For these sizes, one calculation leaves the GPUs largely idle: mean
utilization stays between 11 and \SI{51}{\percent}, and peak memory between 2.8 and
\SI{12.2}{GB} out of \SI{94}{GB}. Time per SCF step grows sublinearly with the number of atoms
because the $k$-point count decreases in larger supercells. r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN costs
0.9 to $7.9\times$ more than PBE for a single point and 1.5 to $15.9\times$ more for a
relaxation. Two GPUs give a speedup of 1.0 to $1.9\times$; distributing $k$-points
(\texttt{KPAR}=2) is the better choice up to about 32 atoms, and distributing bands
(\texttt{KPAR}=1) can overtake it for 64-atom cells with 3 or 4 $k$-points. Timings are
reproducible to within \SI{1}{\percent}. Energies agree with Materials Project to within a few
meV/atom, except for two understood cases: an older Mg PAW potential in the Materials Project
PBE data, and coarse $k$-point sampling of metallic Al. Complete finite-displacement phonon
workflows of Si, Al, and MgO take 0.5 to \SI{1.7}{h} on one GPU and run 1.4 to $1.6\times$ faster on
two. Finally, running several small calculations at once on one GPU recovers much of the idle
capacity, but only with the NVIDIA Multi-Process Service (MPS): eight concurrent single points
raise throughput by 4.1 to $5.8\times$ with MPS, whereas without MPS throughput stays between
0.55 and $1.35\times$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Plane-wave density-functional theory (DFT) codes such as VASP~\cite{Kresse1996} now run on
GPUs, and high-throughput databases such as Materials Project~\cite{Jain2013} rely on
standardized workflows run on thousands of structures, most of them with fewer than 100 atoms.
A small shared node with two recent data-center GPUs raises practical questions for its users:
which system sizes make good use of the hardware, what the standard workflows cost, and how best
to use one or two GPUs. This report answers these questions for the GPU build of VASP 6.5.1 on
such a node, using the Materials Project PBE and r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN input sets unchanged.

This study addresses the following questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How does computing time scale with the number of atoms, from 1 to 64?
  \item How much more does r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN cost than PBE, per self-consistent (SCF)
        step and in total?
  \item How much does a full relaxation cost compared with a single point?
  \item What is gained by going from one to two GPUs, and is it better to distribute
        $k$-points (\texttt{KPAR}=2) or bands and plane waves (\texttt{KPAR}=1)?
  \item How much GPU memory is needed as a function of system size?
  \item Are timings reproducible from one job to the next?
  \item Are the computed energies consistent with those published by Materials Project?
  \item What is the time-to-solution of a complete finite-displacement phonon calculation on one
        and two GPUs?
  \item Can several small calculations share one GPU to recover its idle capacity, with and
        without the NVIDIA Multi-Process Service (MPS)?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Electronic-structure settings}
\label{sec:settings}

All calculations use the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method~\cite{Blochl1994,Kresse1999} as
implemented in VASP~\cite{Kresse1996}. Inputs were generated with the Materials Project input
sets implemented in pymatgen~\cite{Ong2013}, without changing any physical parameter
(Table~\ref{tab:sets}). The PBE sets~\cite{Perdew1996} apply the MP Hubbard $U$
values~\cite{Dudarev1998} to oxides and fluorides of Co, Cr, Fe, Mn, Mo, Ni, and V (none of the
present materials is affected). The r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN sets~\cite{Furness2020} follow the
MP meta-GGA workflow~\cite{Kingsbury2022}, in which the $k$-point spacing is chosen from the MP
band gap. All calculations are spin-polarized (\texttt{ISPIN}=2), with \texttt{PREC}=Accurate
and \texttt{LASPH}=.TRUE. The complete \texttt{INCAR} files are given in Appendix~\ref{app:incar}.

The following deviations from the MP methodology apply identically to every calculation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pseudopotentials.} The PBE\_54 PAW set installed on the machine is used for
        both functionals. MP uses an older PBE set for its GGA calculations, which is identical
        for almost all elements; W\_pv, absent from PBE\_54, would be replaced by W\_sv. Among
        the present materials, only Mg differs: MP used \texttt{Mg\_pv 06Sep2000}, while
        PBE\_54 provides \texttt{Mg\_pv 13Apr2007} (Section~\ref{sec:res-energies}).
  \item \textbf{Output.} Writing of \texttt{WAVECAR}, \texttt{CHGCAR}, \texttt{AECCAR},
        \texttt{LOCPOT}, and \texttt{ELFCAR} is disabled, so that the benchmark measures
        computation rather than I/O.
  \item \textbf{Parallelization tags.} \texttt{NCORE} is removed, since the GPU build forces it
        to 1; \texttt{KPAR} is set by the parallelization variant
        (Section~\ref{sec:variants}).
  \item \textbf{Small $k$-point meshes.} When a mesh has fewer than 4 irreducible $k$-points,
        the tetrahedron method (\texttt{ISMEAR}=$-5$) is replaced by Gaussian smearing
        (\texttt{ISMEAR}=0, \texttt{SIGMA}=\SI{0.05}{eV}), which is also the correction
        applied by custodian in MP workflows.
\end{itemize}

In the supercells, the $k$-point density per atom is kept constant, as in the MP methodology,
so the number of $k$-points decreases as the cell grows. Relaxations start from the MP
structures, which are already relaxed with PBE: PBE relaxations therefore converge in a few
ionic steps, and r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN relaxations need a few more.

\subsection{Measurement protocol}
\label{sec:metrics}

Each calculation runs as one Slurm job-array task launched with
\texttt{mpirun -np $N_\text{GPU}$ --bind-to none vasp\_std}. The following quantities are
recorded:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem size:} number of atoms, \texttt{NKPTS}, \texttt{NBANDS}, \texttt{NPLWV},
        \texttt{NELECT}, \texttt{ISPIN}.
  \item \textbf{Time:} total time (``Elapsed time'' in \texttt{OUTCAR}, and job wall time);
        median time per SCF step (\texttt{LOOP} lines); mean time per ionic step
        (\texttt{LOOP+} lines); numbers of SCF and ionic steps.
  \item \textbf{Memory and utilization:} peak GPU memory and mean GPU utilization, sampled
        every \SI{2}{s} with \texttt{nvidia-smi}; peak host memory.
  \item \textbf{Energy:} energy per atom and its difference from the uncorrected MP energy of
        the same functional, in meV/atom.
  \item \textbf{Context:} other jobs running on the node at start time.
\end{itemize}

The speedup of a two-GPU variant is the ratio of the one-GPU total time to the two-GPU total
time. Reproducibility was assessed by repeating six single-point calculations (Si 8 atoms, Al 32
atoms, and MgO 32 atoms, with PBE and r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN) three times each on one GPU.

The study was carried out in phases, each submitted separately from the smallest to the largest
system (Table~\ref{tab:phases}).

\subsection{Finite-displacement phonons}
\label{sec:phonon-methods}

\sloppy
Harmonic phonons of the primitive cells of Si, Al, and MgO are computed with the
finite-displacement method using phonopy~\cite{Togo2023,Togo2015}. Each material is run as one
automated workflow per Slurm task, with identical inputs on one GPU (\texttt{g1}) and two GPUs
(\texttt{g2k1}, \texttt{KPAR}=1), so that only the hardware changes.

\paragraph{Settings.} \sloppy PBE with PBE\_54 PAW potentials, \texttt{ENCUT}=\SI{520}{eV},
\texttt{PREC}=Accurate, \texttt{EDIFF}=\SI{1e-8}{eV}, \texttt{ADDGRID}=.TRUE.,
\texttt{LREAL}=.FALSE., \texttt{LASPH}=.TRUE., \texttt{ALGO}=Normal, non-spin-polarized.
Gaussian smearing with $\sigma=\SI{0.01}{eV}$ is used for Si and MgO, and first-order
Methfessel--Paxton smearing with $\sigma=\SI{0.2}{eV}$ for Al. $\Gamma$-centered $k$-meshes
are built with $N_i = \max(1, \operatorname{round}(L\,|\mathbf{b}_i|))$, where $\mathbf{b}_i$ are
the reciprocal lattice vectors (without the $2\pi$ factor) and $L = \SI{50}{\angstrom}$ for
insulators and \SI{80}{\angstrom} for the metal.

\paragraph{Workflow.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tight relaxation} of the primitive cell (\texttt{ISIF}=3,
        \texttt{EDIFFG}=\SI{-1e-3}{eV/\angstrom}). The relaxation is restarted from the
        previous \texttt{CONTCAR}, which resets the plane-wave basis, until a pass completes in
        at most two ionic steps (at most four passes).
  \item \textbf{Born effective charges and $\varepsilon_\infty$} for polar materials (MgO) from
        density-functional perturbation theory (\texttt{LEPSILON}), with a finite-field
        fallback (\texttt{LCALCEPS}). They are used for the non-analytical term correction.
  \item \textbf{Force calculations} for the symmetry-irreducible displacements
        (\SI{0.01}{\angstrom}) in two diagonal supercell sizes, to check convergence
        (Table~\ref{tab:phonon-cells}).
  \item \textbf{Post-processing:} symmetrized force constants, band structure along the
        seekpath path~\cite{Hinuma2017}, density of states on a $30^3$ $q$-mesh, and
        thermodynamic properties from 0 to \SI{1000}{K}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Concurrent runs on one GPU}
\label{sec:conc-methods}
Four single points of the one-GPU campaign were rerun as batches of $N$ identical copies sharing
one GPU: PBE Si (8 atoms) and MgO (32 atoms), and r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN Al (32 atoms) with
$N=2$, 4, and 8; r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN Si (64 atoms) with $N=2$ and 4, limited by the
$\sim$\SI{28}{GB} of host memory available per GPU. The inputs are those of the \texttt{g1} runs,
unchanged. Each batch runs in a single Slurm job with one GPU and 8 cores, since Slurm does not
share a GPU between jobs on this node; each copy is one MPI rank with
\texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS}=1. Two modes are compared. Without MPS, the CUDA contexts of the $N$
processes share the GPU by time slicing, the default behavior. With MPS, a daemon private to the
job lets kernels from the $N$ processes run on the GPU at the same time. A run of one copy under
MPS checks that the daemon itself costs nothing.

The throughput gain of a batch is $N\,t_\text{ref}/T_\text{batch}$, where $t_\text{ref}$ is the
mean one-GPU time of the calculation run alone (Section~\ref{sec:res-scaling}) and
$T_\text{batch}$ is the time from the first start to the last end of the batch; the ideal value
is $N$. The slowdown of each calculation is its time in the batch divided by $t_\text{ref}$.

\paragraph{Scaling.} Times are short: every PBE calculation finishes in less than \SI{5.5}{min},
and the longest calculation, the r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN relaxation of 64-atom MgO, takes
\SI{48}{min}. Fitting $t_\text{SCF} \propto N^{\alpha}$ between 8 and 64 atoms for Si and MgO
gives $\alpha = 0.6$--0.85 with PBE and 0.85--0.95 with r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN: the time per
step grows more slowly than the number of atoms. This is mostly a consequence of the MP
$k$-point density, which is fixed per reciprocal volume, so that the number of irreducible
$k$-points drops from 10--20 at 8 atoms to 4 at 64 atoms. Per $k$-point, the exponent is
1.25--1.4, still far below the $N^2$--$N^3$ behavior expected once the GPU is saturated. For the smallest
cells, times are almost flat (about \SI{1}{s} per PBE SCF step for Si and Al up to 8 atoms):
fixed overheads dominate.

\subsection{Relaxation versus single point}
\label{sec:res-relax}
Table~\ref{tab:relax} compares relaxations with single points. Since the MP structures are
already relaxed with PBE, PBE relaxations take 1 to 3 ionic steps and cost 0.9 to $1.9\times$ a

\subsection{Phonons}
\label{sec:res-phonons}
Figure~\ref{fig:phonon-dispersions} shows the PBE phonon dispersions computed on one GPU
(\texttt{g1}) for both supercell sizes. No imaginary frequency appears: on the $30^3$ $q$-mesh,
the lowest frequency away from $\Gamma$ is positive for all three materials, and the acoustic
branches go to zero at $\Gamma$. The highest optical frequency of Si at $\Gamma$ is
\SI{503}{cm^{-1}}; the Al spectrum extends up to \SI{322}{cm^{-1}}. For MgO, the non-analytical
term correction (Born charge $Z^*=1.99$, $\varepsilon_\infty=3.24$) splits the optical modes at
$\Gamma$ into TO (\SI{371}{cm^{-1}}) and LO (\SI{680}{cm^{-1}}).

The two supercell sizes give almost the same dispersions. The largest differences are on
zone-boundary acoustic branches: \SI{18}{cm^{-1}} for the transverse acoustic mode of Si at X
(\SI{129}{} to \SI{147}{cm^{-1}}), \SI{18}{cm^{-1}} for MgO and \SI{8}{cm^{-1}} for Al.
At \SI{300}{K}, the heat capacity changes by less than \SI{0.05}{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} between
the two sizes, so the smaller supercell is enough for thermodynamic properties, while the
larger one is needed for zone-boundary frequencies. The \texttt{g2k1} runs give the same
frequencies as \texttt{g1} to within \SI{0.02}{cm^{-1}}.

\section{Discussion and Recommendations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Are these GPUs suited to VASP?}
\label{sec:disc-suitability}

The results do not show that the H100 GPUs are unsuited to VASP. They show that systems of 1 to
64 atoms leave these GPUs largely underused.

\paragraph{What the measurements show.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Absolute times are short.} A PBE single point on 64-atom MgO takes \SI{163}{s}
        on one GPU, and a full r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN relaxation of the same cell takes
        \SI{48}{min}.
  \item \textbf{The GPUs are lightly loaded.} On one GPU, the mean utilization reported by
        \texttt{nvidia-smi} ranges from 10 to \SI{50}{\percent}, and the peak GPU memory from
        3 to \SI{12}{GB} out of \SI{94}{GB}. The utilization metric is the fraction of time
        during which at least one kernel is running, sampled every \SI{2}{s}; it does not
        measure how much of the device each kernel occupies, so the actual occupancy is lower
        still.
  \item \textbf{Two GPUs bring a modest gain.} The speedup ranges from 1.0 to $1.9\times$,
        generally grows with system size, and vanishes for the 1-atom cell with \texttt{KPAR}=1
        (Section~\ref{sec:res-2gpu}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation.} This is the expected behavior of plane-wave DFT on GPUs. For small
cells, fast Fourier transforms and dense matrix operations are too small to fill an H100, and
run time is dominated by latencies: kernel launches, synchronizations, host--device transfers,
and, with two GPUs, inter-GPU communication. The GPU port becomes efficient for systems with a
few hundred atoms, or with many bands and $k$-points.

\paragraph{Limits of the present study.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No CPU reference.} Only the GPU (OpenACC) build of VASP is installed on the
        node, so no CPU-only timings were measured. The present data therefore cannot support
        any claim about GPU versus CPU performance.
  \item \textbf{The CPU was not benchmarked.} Each calculation uses one MPI rank per GPU with
        \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS}=1: a single core does work out of the 16 allocated per GPU,
        which is the normal setup for the GPU build.
  \item \textbf{No large systems.} The largest cell has 64 atoms, below the size range where
        the GPU port is expected to pay off.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Recommendations for users of the node}
\label{sec:disc-recommendations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item For cells of up to 64 atoms, one GPU per calculation is sufficient. Running two
        independent calculations, one per GPU, yields more throughput than one calculation on
        two GPUs.
  \item When two GPUs are used, prefer \texttt{KPAR}=2 for cells with many irreducible
        $k$-points (here, up to about 32 atoms). With only 3 or 4 $k$-points (64-atom cells),
        \texttt{KPAR}=1 can be as fast or faster, especially when the $k$-points do not split
        evenly between the two GPUs.
  \item Memory is not a constraint in this size range: the H100s leave ample room for much
        larger systems.
  \item For many small independent calculations (screening, phonon displacements), run 4 to 8
        of them on the same GPU under MPS: throughput increases 2.5 to 5.8 times
        (Section~\ref{sec:res-conc}). Without MPS, never run more than one calculation per GPU.
        On this node, MPS must be started inside the job, with pipe and log directories private
        to the job, as in the scripts of this study; the number of copies is limited by the
        host memory allocated per GPU.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Further tests}
\label{sec:disc-further}

Two tests would complete the assessment of the hardware (the third one initially planned,
concurrent runs under MPS, is reported in Section~\ref{sec:res-conc}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{CPU reference.} Build a CPU version of VASP 6.5.1 and rerun a subset of the
        calculations on 16 and 32 cores.
  \item \textbf{Larger systems.} Extend the Si and MgO series to 128, 256, and 512 atoms, where
        the GPU port should be efficient.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On this dual-H100 node, the GPU build of VASP 6.5.1 runs the standard Materials Project PBE and
r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN workflows for 1- to 64-atom cells correctly and quickly: all 168
calculations completed, energies are consistent with MP once pseudopotential and $k$-point
differences are accounted for, one-GPU and two-GPU energies are identical, and timings are
reproducible to within \SI{1}{\percent}. PBE single points and relaxations take seconds to a
few minutes, and r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN relaxations up to \SI{48}{min}.

In this size range, however, one calculation uses only a small fraction of a GPU: utilization
stays below about \SI{50}{\percent}, memory below \SI{13}{GB}, and the time per SCF step grows
sublinearly with the number of atoms. Two GPUs shorten a calculation by a factor of 1.0 to
1.9 only, so for small cells running one calculation per GPU gives more throughput. When two
GPUs are used, \texttt{KPAR}=2 is preferable when there are many $k$-points, and
\texttt{KPAR}=1 can be better when there are only 3 or 4. Complete phonon workflows of small
cells take less than two hours on one GPU. Most of the idle capacity can be recovered by running
several calculations on the same GPU under MPS: eight concurrent single points give 4 to 6 times
the throughput of one, while without MPS concurrent runs are no faster, or even slower, than
running them one after the other. Larger systems remain to be tested.

\paragraph{Tags that depend on the structure or the variant.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{KPAR}: 1 for \texttt{g1} and \texttt{g2k1}, 2 for \texttt{g2k2}
        (Section~\ref{sec:variants}).
  \item \texttt{MAGMOM}: $N\times0.6$, with $N$ the number of atoms (MP default initial moment).
  \item \texttt{EDIFF} (PBE only): $5\times10^{-5}\times N$~eV, i.e.\ from \num{5e-5} (Al, 1 atom)
        to \SI{3.2e-3}{eV} (64 atoms). The r\textsuperscript{2}SCAN sets use
        \SI{1e-5}{eV} for every cell.
  \item Smearing, PBE: \texttt{ISMEAR}=$-5$, \texttt{SIGMA}=0.05 everywhere, except Al 64 atoms
        (fewer than 4 irreducible $k$-points), where \texttt{ISMEAR}=0.

        and \SI{0.22}{\angstrom^{-1}} (Al, metal). For Al, \texttt{SIGMA}=0.2, with
        \texttt{ISMEAR}=2 (Methfessel--Paxton) in relaxations; the single points keep
        \texttt{ISMEAR}=$-5$.
